H₂-Retrofit Ireland — Critical Investor Analysis

Prepared: March 2026  ·  Role: Critical external investor perspective  ·  Scope: 50 homes + 1 ha greenhouse, West of Ireland

1. Top 5 Risks That Kill This Project

Risk 1: Your feedstock is not a resource — it's a scheduling problem.

Curtailment in Ireland is managed by EirGrid through constraint payments to wind operators. You do not have a right to that energy. You need signed offtake agreements with wind farm operators willing to divert curtailed output to your electrolyzer on short notice, at a price, in real time. No wind operator has an incentive to manage that complexity for a 50-home buyer. As Ireland adds interconnectors and battery storage, curtailment rates drop — your cheapest input disappears as the grid improves.

Risk 2: Hydrogen storage near 50 homes in rural Ireland will not get planning permission easily.

Compressed hydrogen storage is classified as a major hazard installation above certain thresholds. Irish planning law and the Health and Safety Authority's COMAH regulations will require exclusion zones, third-party risk assessments, and potentially a full EIA. No Irish local authority has approved a residential-adjacent hydrogen storage facility. You have no regulatory pathway demonstrated here — just an assumption that it works.

Risk 3: District heating infrastructure CAPEX is the invisible killer.

To deliver heat to 50 homes you need a district heating network — insulated piping, heat exchangers, controls, metering. In Ireland, essentially zero existing district heating infrastructure exists outside Dublin pilot schemes. Installing this in dispersed West of Ireland housing (not high-density) could easily cost €1.5M–€3M before you've bought a single electrolyzer. This cost is absent from the model.

Risk 4: Three revenue streams that conflict with each other.

Heat supply needs continuous thermal output. Grid balancing needs dispatchable, on-demand response. Greenhouse heating needs predictable, sustained low-grade heat. An intermittent, curtailment-fed electrolyzer cannot simultaneously optimise for all three. When curtailment is high you have surplus; when the grid is balanced you have nothing. You cannot commit to grid services contracts (DS3/FFR in Ireland) without firm capacity guarantees you cannot provide.

Risk 5: 50 homes is not a viable commercial scale — it's a demonstration priced as a business.

The economics of electrolysis, hydrogen compression, and district heating only become defensible at multi-MW scale. At this scale, CAPEX per delivered kWh of heat will be 5–10× more expensive than a heat pump. Investors don't fund demonstrations without a scale-up roadmap and a funder — typically public money — absorbing pilot losses.

2. Challenging the Assumptions

€0.03–0.05/kWh curtailed energy cost

This is not a market price — it's a reference figure from academic literature. To actually procure curtailed wind you need a bilateral contract with a wind operator. Those operators receive constraint payments from EirGrid for not generating. Your offer must beat that, and you must accept energy only when they have surplus, not when you need it. Realistically, contracted curtailed energy with dispatch flexibility built in is closer to €0.05–0.08/kWh after transaction costs.

Hydrogen production cost

At €0.04/kWh input, with a 65% efficient electrolyzer, you're at ~€6.15/kg in energy cost alone. Add CAPEX amortisation for a small-scale PEM (€1,500–3,000/kW installed), compression, storage, and O&M — you are producing hydrogen at €10–18/kg at this scale. Natural gas heating in Ireland costs the equivalent of €1.5–2/kg hydrogen in delivered energy terms. You are not competitive on heat. You are not competitive on anything except the narrative.

Greenhouse waste heat viability

The waste heat from an electrolyzer sized for 50 homes (200–500 kW input) yields roughly 60–150 kW of low-grade waste heat when operating. A 1-hectare commercial greenhouse in Ireland requires 300–600 kW of continuous heating in winter. The math doesn't work unless the electrolyzer runs continuously — which defeats the curtailment-only premise.

Revenue from agriculture

A 1-hectare greenhouse in Ireland competing against Dutch and Spanish imports earns €100K–€300K/year gross in a good year for tomatoes or herbs, with 60–70% cost ratios. This is a separate agricultural business with its own risks — not a hydrogen project revenue stream.

3. What Looks Unrealistic

  • "Hydrogen as storage" is listed as both a technology and a revenue stream. Grid balancing services require reconversion — you need fuel cells or turbines to discharge. Neither is in the system description.
  • Grid balancing services (EirGrid DS3/FFR) require firm availability commitments and ramp-rate guarantees that a curtailment-dependent system cannot structurally provide without backup capacity.
  • West of Ireland grid connection for an industrial-scale electrolyzer load means long distribution lines, weak grid nodes, and connection costs of €300K–€800K. Not in the model.
  • Electrolyzer stack replacement every 60,000–80,000 hours (~7–9 years) is a major recurring CAPEX routinely excluded from optimistic project models.

Bottom Line

This is a technically interesting concept built on a stack of unvalidated assumptions at a scale too small to be commercial and too large to be a cheap pilot. The three revenue streams look diversified on paper but are structurally in tension with each other given the intermittent input. The project as described would likely deliver hydrogen at 5–10× the cost of any competing heat source.

The path forward is not to make it sound better. Spend €50K–€100K on feasibility work — actual site studies, real vendor quotes, a planning pre-application — before asking anyone for serious capital. Right now you have a hypothesis, not a project.
