AGRO INTEGRATION

How it works: Electrolysis splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity. The process is 60–70% efficient — meaning 20–30% of input energy is released as heat. That heat is not wasted: it is captured and directed to greenhouse heating. The hydrogen produced is used for backup space heating in homes and agricultural facilities.

Energy flow: Wind electricity → Electrolyser → Hydrogen (fuel) + Heat (20–30% of input) → Greenhouse heating + Home backup heating

Why it matters:

→Year-round food production. Greenhouses eliminate seasonal growing limits, producing vegetables in winter when field crops are not viable.
→Ireland imports most of its vegetables. Over 80% of fresh vegetables consumed in Ireland are imported. Local greenhouse production directly reduces that dependency.
→Stable energy demand. Greenhouses require consistent low-grade heat — a predictable baseload that improves electrolyser utilisation and project economics.
This is not only an energy system.
It is a local energy and food production model.

CRITICAL INVESTOR ANALYSIS

Independent risk assessment — West of Ireland, 50 homes + 1 ha greenhouse

TOP 5 RISKS
1. Feedstock is not a resource — it's a scheduling problem
Curtailment is managed by EirGrid through constraint payments. You need signed offtake agreements with wind operators willing to divert output in real time. No wind operator has an incentive to manage that complexity for a 50-home buyer. As Ireland adds interconnectors, curtailment drops — your cheapest input disappears.
2. Hydrogen storage near homes will not get planning permission easily
Compressed hydrogen above certain thresholds is a major hazard installation under COMAH regulations. Exclusion zones, third-party risk assessments, and possibly a full EIA required. No Irish local authority has approved a residential-adjacent hydrogen storage facility.
3. District heating CAPEX is the invisible killer
Delivering heat to 50 dispersed rural homes requires insulated piping, heat exchangers, controls, and metering. Ireland has almost no district heating infrastructure outside Dublin. Cost: €1.5M–€3M before buying a single electrolyzer.
4. Three revenue streams that conflict with each other
Heat supply needs continuous output. Grid balancing needs dispatchable on-demand response. Greenhouse heating needs sustained low-grade heat. A curtailment-fed electrolyzer cannot optimise for all three. DS3/FFR contracts require firm availability guarantees you cannot provide.
5. 50 homes is a demonstration priced as a business
Electrolysis and district heating economics only work at multi-MW scale. At this scale CAPEX per delivered kWh is 5–10× more expensive than a heat pump. Investors don't fund demonstrations without a scale-up roadmap and public funding absorbing pilot losses.

BOTTOM LINE
This is a technically interesting concept built on unvalidated assumptions at a scale too small to be commercial and too large to be a cheap pilot. The project as described would deliver hydrogen at 5–10× the cost of any competing heat source. Spend €50K–€100K on feasibility work first — real vendor quotes, site studies, planning pre-application — before asking anyone for serious capital. Right now this is a hypothesis, not a project.
